# The Empire and The Quiet — Lore Précis for Development Reference

> "If magic existed in a real world, that stuff would be regulated."

## Core Concept

A continent of approximately 9.5 million people governed by a sophisticated empire now in its 1072nd year (the current year is approximately 1072 AC). The world's single most important structural feature: magical ability (resonance/rarebirth) is completely random with no hereditary pattern. This prevents magical dynasties and makes the entire political system possible. Approximately 1 in 300 people are rareborn, with 8 thread types each appearing in roughly 1 in 2400 people. Prismborn — full spectrum amplifiers — appear at approximately 1 in a million. Of note - in this world fantastical eye colours appear as well, but these mean nothing besides being pretty.

## The Eight Threads

Voidborn/Nullborn (black hair) — dampening and cancellation. Bloommarked (green) — biological growth and organic chemistry. Tidebound (blue) — water and currents. Firelit (red/copper) — heat and fire. Galeheart (white/silver) — air and wind. Stonewrought (brown/grey) — structural and geological. Starlit (gold) — light manipulation. Rosebound (pink) — emotional cohesion and biological maintenance. Hair color is the visible marker of thread type from birth, making concealment difficult. (The correct term is "Bloommarked," never "Bloommark.")

Modern resonance has exactly these eight recognized threads. Pre-war and archaic records sometimes describe impossible or extinct effects — death reversal ("suspended capital sentences pending resonance recovery"), command over monsters, and stranger things — but these are rare, disputed, politically dangerous to discuss, and not available as normal present-day powers. Scholars file them under "lost threads"; the ruins and old shrines are full of hints, and nobody credible claims to understand them.

### Voidborn and Nullborn

**Voidborn** and **nullborn** are regional names for the same Thread (black hair): rareborn whose resonance produces states of nullification and noninteraction. Crucially, neither term means commonborn, powerless, or naturally without resonance — a persistent confusion worth heading off. A null space is not merely a place where resonance happens to be absent; it is an actively sustained resonant condition in which some ordinary interaction has been prevented. Depending on the practitioner's ability and training, electrical charge may cease to flow, vibrations may fail to make sound, heat or force may stop transferring between objects, chemical reactions may be interrupted, or matter may temporarily fail to interact as expected. Many effects commonly regarded as "magic" are therefore voidborn manifestations in their own right, rather than the cancellation of some other rareborn's power.

Suppressing another Thread is only one application. A voidborn might extinguish a firelit flame, silence a ringing instrument, isolate a dangerous mechanism, or stop moving components from transferring force. Powerful applications can be extremely dangerous: nullifying the wrong interaction, or doing so unevenly, may cause structural failure, uncontrolled movement, or injury. As with every Thread, void is not inherited — voidborn children appear unpredictably among commonborn families, exactly like any other rareborn.

By 1072 AC, aetherrail operation is the most common voidborn occupation in the Empire, and it has transformed their public image. Voidborn were once associated principally with uncanny silence, failed mechanisms, and frightening violations of ordinary physical behaviour; they are now also associated with one of the Empire's most prestigious and indispensable modern professions (see The Aetherrail).

## History

The Century War was, in the broadest terms, a war of commonborn against rareborn, and it ended — after a fashion — at Year 0. What follows is the best surviving reconstruction, and accepted Imperial history, rather than perfectly documented fact: powerful rareborn factions first rose to overthrow the old kingdoms, then fractured and largely destroyed one another. Organized rareborn assaults mostly ceased, but the resonant storms, monsters, unsafe rivers, and hostile ecological changes they had loosed did not. By Year 0 civilization had contracted into the defended country around the two cities still standing, Lumina and Halcyra, with perhaps only 300,000 to 400,000 people left alive. Those two cities led a unification compact, joined or merely recognized by whatever remained nearby: surviving town councils, military commanders, a few intact monarchs, and refugee rulers whose territories no longer existed. Year 0 was thus less a confirmed, decisive victory than a declaration by exhausted survivors who needed the war to be over — a wager that civilization could stop retreating. The surviving realm anchored on Halcyra corresponds to Cyrren, the region around the Cyrmere inland sea; these are territories, not additional capitals.

A postwar purge began shortly after Year 0, killing most surviving rareborn, collaborators and surrendered practitioners alike. In 3 AC the young Empire went further, establishing automatic standing death warrants for any child born with the condition; these stood until the Accord of 700 AC. The early Empire justified the policy as public safety after an extinction-level catastrophe — a justification recorded here without endorsement.

Then came the long work of reclamation: reopening roads and rivers, refounding abandoned settlements, and clearing land still held by monsters. Two episodes are remembered. Greyhaven was retaken in 20 AC after being overrun by enormous wolf-like creatures driven by two ancient practitioners of a lost beast-binding thread; the controllers' preserved remains were found in the ruins, though whether age or their own creatures killed them is unknown. The Istral family professionalized the trade — organized hunters, engineers, traps, and contracts — most famously in a six-year clearing of the Red Fen that cost roughly 430 lives and reopened a vital corridor. Recovery was slow: about 800,000 by 220 AC, roughly three million by 700 AC. Across these Silent Centuries the world repopulated as a more or less ordinary medieval one, while rebel factions and rareborn concealers slowly regathered — the pressure that finally produced the Accord.

The Accord of Threads (a long bureaucratic title in law, this by-name in common speech) was signed in 700 AC, its principal political architect the Concordant Arven Sael. Famously it never uses the words rareborn, resonance, or magic: it recognizes only "the condition," as something persistent, involuntary, and "observable through outward and measurable signs." It rescinds the automatic death warrants (while preserving a death penalty for non-compliance), requires registration and reporting, authorizes compulsory evaluation and instruction, and permits property ownership under extensive safeguards. Article VIII provides that ambiguity resolves in favor of public order — the document's immune system.

The first Academy students arrived in 701 AC, under direct military supervision. On the Accord's first anniversary, about seventy of them held the first Lantern Walk: six carefully chosen speakers praised the Empire, Sael, the Council, and the Accord, and criticized none of the old laws. Its radical content was simply that rareborn people appeared in public — alive, undisguised, unafraid. The observance grew into Accord Day, and lanterns became central to rareborn cultural life. This is distinct from Lantern Night, the much later Academy graduation ball (see The Academy System).

Following this becoming law, resonance users have become a sometimes resented, often cloistered, part of society. They must be registered shortly after birth, be educated from ages 12-18 at special academies and very often graduate to work directly for the Empire. Especially over the last 150 years, resonant abilities are leading to a growth in technologies such as resonance-driven rail ("aetherrail"), hardier crops, and more controllable weather.

Nobody remembers what went on before The Century War - certainly society had existed for thousands of years, and there are shrines to old religions, signs of lost threads, and ruins in many places, but nobody alive knows what these are - the war burned down the history that came before it.

Though binding in law from 700 AC, the Accord's enforcement reached the outer provinces unevenly. For generations after ratification, frontier practice lagged central law: registration offices were distant, provincial administration was thin, and rural rareborn families sometimes still hid as their grandparents had. Later instruments — the Integration Accords (which extended working enforcement and registry infrastructure to the outer provinces), the Reform (a recodification of the Accord code; older provisions survive only as "pre-Reform commentary" in registry archives), and successive registry expansions — brought frontier practice progressively closer to the written law. The Empire's thousandth year (1000 AC) was marked by the Millennial Accord, part celebration and part omnibus reaffirmation of imperial law; provincial elders still date their lives by it. When someone in the fens says a person was "born before the Integration Accords," they mean this long, uneven rollout — not that the original Accord did not exist.

Archives occasionally surface "Classification Registers" dated to the decades before 700 AC — before the Accord created lawful registration. What pre-Accord "classification" actually meant is deliberately ambiguous and quietly sinister: kill lists, containment ledgers, provincial intelligence records, proto-registry documents, or some mixture, many later folded without comment into Accord archives. Serious scholars handle such volumes carefully, and district offices prefer not to discuss them.

## Political Structure

The Council of Thirty-Three — the Three Elevens: eleven hereditary noble seats, eleven elected seats (one per region, five year terms), eleven Wisdom Council seats (selected by existing members after 33 years Imperial service, serve until death). The Concordant is the closest they have to a president, though really they are more like a speaker of the house- they vote only to break ties. The Wisdom Council sides with nobles approximately 90% of the time. The elected eleven represent 95% of the population. This representation gap is the system's central unresolved tension.

Eight kingdoms with their own rulers send representatives to the noble eleven. Twin capitals: Lumina (magical/cultural capital, limestone bluff above the Lume river) and Halcyra (administrative capital, western shore of the Cyrmere inland sea).

## The Quiet

700 AC to the present day (approximately 1072 AC) — some 370 years of managed peace. Population roughly tripled across these centuries — to about nine million by 1040 AC and near 9.5 million today. Literacy reached 70% continentally through the Instruction to Twelve Act — that is the formal title; ordinary people call it the Education Act of 871. It provides publicly funded grade schooling up to age 12, open to any family that wishes to enroll its children, rareborn and commonborn alike. The aetherrail — powered by Stonewrought, Galeheart, and Voidborn practitioners — opened its first operational Imperial line, Halcyra to Lumina (the twin capital cities - the 2 surviving cities mentioned above), in approximately 1020 AC: the technology is only about 52 years old, the newest and most visible fruit of the Quiet. Now 11 lines, 47 stations — all of it in the richer northeast. No line or stop serves Calwick or Drenwick; the nearest railhead is three towns east of Calwick (two towns beyond Drenwick), reached by road and coach.

## Economics

Currency: in everyday use across the provinces, the coin is simply "gold" — wages, tolls, shop prices, rewards, and ledgers are all quoted in gold pieces (the "g" of every counter and pay ticket). This is the currency ordinary people use and the game UI shows. Formal Imperial accounting retains older bureaucratic denominations (marks, crowns, sovereigns) in central ledgers and instruments of credit, but a fen clerk can go years without seeing one written down. The Kethrin (a noble family) invented standardized letters of credit and effectively operates as the continental central bank. Do not derive salary or price scales that contradict in-game amounts — as reference points, a canal toll is 1 gold, a potion runs about 30 gold, and a hired district investigation pays 50-200 gold.

The Bracelet, developed around 930–940 AC through combined Rosebound and Bloommarked work, is a contraceptive worn on the wrist; a properly made one stays active for roughly forty years. Pregnancy is prevented if either sexual partner wears one, and if a pregnant person begins wearing one the pregnancy ends painlessly within a few days — even at later stages. The dominant cultures do not generally treat fetal life as possessing independent legal or moral personhood, so the function is not inherently scandalous; the controversy is over access, not legality. Bracelets are free to Academy students (voluntarily, never compulsorily), common among nobles and wealthy civic families, expensive for administrators and merchants, and largely out of reach for peasants except through unreliable imitations and bootlegs. The upshot is that affluent people — and many Academy graduates, whatever their original class — can separate sex from reproduction, delay marriage, and plan heirs, while lower-class courtship stays far more consequential. Reproductive freedom thus becomes a class marker in itself, feeding the stereotype of nobles and Academy graduates as libertines; for most people it is simply not part of ordinary life.

## The Aetherrail

The aetherrail is the most revolutionary transport technology of the modern Empire: where a working line exists, journeys that once took months of river travel, coastal sailing, and dangerous roads are completed in days. (Its first operational line, Halcyra to Lumina, opened around 1020 AC; by 1072 there are 11 lines and 47 stations, all in the richer northeast — see The Quiet.) Its trains use neither steam nor electricity. Operation depends on the continuous cooperation of three Threads: stone, wind, and void.

**Stonewrought engineers** build and maintain the rails, which must be far more precise than any ordinary road — gauge, curvature, gradient, foundations, and station approaches all held within narrow tolerances. A train running under heavy nullification has surrendered much of the friction and mechanical resistance that would ordinarily help constrain its movement, so a small misalignment, uneven rail height, damaged joint, or subsiding foundation can turn catastrophic. Wind and void operators can work safely only because the stonewrought have made the train's physical path completely predictable.

At departure, **voidborn operators** suppress friction and resistance across the train's relevant wheels, axles, and rail interfaces, and **galeheart (wind) operators** apply the force that propels it. Removing friction does not remove mass or inertia — momentum must still be acquired gradually — so starting is slow but relatively straightforward. Stopping is far harder: the voidborn must begin restoring ordinary friction and resistance at precisely the correct distance, letting the train shed its momentum gradually and uniformly. If resistance returns too quickly, passengers may be injured and the train or track damaged; if unevenly, different wheels or cars decelerate at different rates; if too late, the train overruns the station or requires a dangerous emergency stop. The correct braking depends on speed, total mass, cargo distribution, gradient, weather, track condition, and the individual train's behaviour, and may begin kilometres before the station. Hence the railway saying:

> **Any fool can start a train, but only training can stop one.**

The expression is now used well beyond the railway, for ambitious people who begin undertakings without understanding how to conclude them safely.

Because acceleration and braking are lengthy, delicate, and expensive, aetherrail lines have relatively few stops, and stations require long, meticulously maintained approach corridors rather than merely a platform beside the track. The network consumes scarce rareborn labour with every departure and demands permanent stonewrought maintenance even when no trains are running. Trains primarily carry passengers and light, valuable, or urgent cargo — Imperial dispatches, letters, medicines, precision goods, small perishables, personal baggage — and not grain, timber, ore, stone, bulk wine, or other heavy freight. Suppressing a heavily loaded train's resistance is possible, but wind must still impart momentum to its enormous mass and void must later bring that mass safely to rest; the required force, personnel, braking distance, and risk make routine heavy rail freight prohibitively expensive.

Rivers, lakes, and canals therefore remain the commercial arteries of the Empire. Water carries the material substance of civilization; the aetherrail carries people, information, and urgency. A merchant may travel by rail with contracts and samples while the goods themselves follow weeks later by barge. Passage remains beyond the ordinary means of much of the population: its habitual riders are nobles, wealthy merchants, senior administrators, and people travelling at institutional expense — Academy students, Imperial personnel, and emergency specialists ride despite lacking personal wealth because the state pays their passage. An ordinary family might save for years to fund one momentous journey. The aetherrail has thus made the continent small for the Empire before making it small for its people: someone may live within sight of a line and never board a train, while officials and the wealthy cross in days distances their grandparents needed a season to travel.

## The Academy System

Formal Academy attendance is compulsory for rareborn youth. The younger cohort, ages twelve to fifteen, attends the Junior Academy, whose formal geographic location is the Ludmereson crater — universally "the Bowl" — at the Brinne–Lume confluence; it was thrown up in only five years to relieve overcrowding without splitting the system into unequal regional academies. Ages sixteen to eighteen then attend the older Senior Academy, between Lumina and Halcyra. (Article IV of the Accord established "an Academy," singular, and for centuries the Senior campus was the whole system; the Junior Academy came later, when the rareborn student population outgrew a single site.)

Before age twelve, rareborn children attend ordinary public grade school alongside commonborn children — the Instruction to Twelve Act school system — or no school at all, at their family's discretion. Some families, especially wealthy or status-conscious ones, enroll children far earlier in private schools, including rareborn Academy-prep schools in the major cities. Alecton is the best known of these prep schools: private, expensive, early-entry (children as young as two), with small cohorts of a dozen or so per year. Prep-school testing measures control, strength, temperament, discipline, and administrative risk — never basic thread type, which the hair announces at birth and the registry records shortly after.

The Academy has also become one of the continent's great engines of youth culture, its fashions and music carrying well beyond rareborn circles. The current craze is the "Shut Up and Kiss Me" dance, set to the opening movement of Erik Jontek's otherwise obscure Resonant Symphony No. 2.

The Academy system is simultaneously a genuine educational institution and a sophisticated behavioral observation system. Lantern Night — the graduation ball, and not to be confused with the annual Accord Day Lantern Walk (see History) — uses intoxicating Elixirs that lower inhibitions while faculty observe. Students who figure this out are not wrong and are not free of it. The surveillance and the genuine gift are the same thing.

### Lantern Nights and Lantern Lovers

Academy graduation celebrations are **Lantern Nights**, taking their name and symbolism from the first public Lantern Walk held after the Accord permitted rareborn children to live and study openly (see History). The Academy is unusually conducive to romance. Rareborn are a small, conspicuous minority everywhere in the Empire, and many students arrive having been the only rareborn child in their village, or one of very few in a whole town. At the Academy their most unusual characteristic is suddenly ordinary, among classmates who understand registration, involuntary manifestations, public scrutiny, frightened families, and the expectations placed on rareborn adults without needing any of it explained.

Relationships form within a Thread or across Threads. Same-Thread couples may share an immediate understanding of one another's physical experiences, difficulties, and habits; mixed-Thread couples share the broader rareborn experience while bringing substantially different abilities and perspectives to the relationship. Neither pattern is considered inherently superior, though students and alumni maintain countless playful opinions about supposed Thread compatibility.

A pair who meet at the Academy and remain together after leaving are called **Lantern Lovers** — roughly equivalent to "school sweethearts," but carrying stronger rareborn implications. Graduation scatters students across the Empire through employment, placement, and family obligation, and a relationship that survives the transition back into ordinary society preserves something of the belonging the couple first felt at the Academy. The term does not necessarily imply marriage, nor is it limited by sex or by Thread; it generally applies only retrospectively, once the relationship has demonstrably endured. Current students may joke that an inseparable pair intends to become Lantern Lovers, but graduation and separation are the real test. Academy romances fail as often and as messily as youthful romances anywhere; nevertheless, Lantern Lovers are statistically more common than enduring relationships formed at ordinary schools — shared minority experience, residential education, an intense alumni culture, and the rarity of ever meeting many other rareborn all encourage unusually strong attachments. To call an older couple Lantern Lovers is therefore affectionate, faintly teasing, and genuinely admiring: it means not simply that they met while young, but that they found one another during the brief span when neither of them was strange, and carried that belonging with them into the wider world.

That belonging has a shadow. Plenty of commonborn resent the rareborn and remain deeply suspicious of them — of the registration and Imperial employment that set them apart, of the thread-colour hair that announces them at a glance, of powers a neighbour cannot see coming. A pair of Lantern Lovers — two rareborn keeping their own charmed company — can draw that suspicion doubly, read as clannish, privileged, or simply other. Some commonborn, of course, are perfectly easy about all of it. But the ordinary rareborn adult, paired with their own kind or not, generally lives with a low, steady wariness from a good part of the population, and knows it.

## The Rebel Factions

The Reclaimers — populist, commonfolk underrepresentation - rural anger, anti-elitist and sometimes communist. The Unshackled Flame — rareborn supremacists, radical faction believes the Century War was a failed revolution and the wrong side won. These two factions - if they could band together, could threaten the empire, however because they are at loggerheads with each other, they are never able to.

## The World

The Empire governs "The Continent" — the name for the large landmass that, with its surrounding seas and islands, comprises the known world. It lies in the southern hemisphere, so climate generally warms toward the north and cools toward the south. The continent runs roughly 4,800 kilometres north–south and about 3,200 kilometres at its widest, and it angles southeast toward its southern end: the land at its far southeast reaches substantially colder latitudes than the islands off its southwest, even though both fall along the same broad band of the map.

A permanent resonance storm runs near the equator, north of the continent, and prevents meaningful travel farther north; how it works is not explained. From the northern coasts it reads less as a literal wall than as a persistent flickering of atmospheric light or discoloration above the horizon. The far north, up against that storm, is hot and tropical: its islands grow bananas, and a rare few grow sugar cane (see C1). Refined northern sugar is scarce enough that the finest grades can be worth more by weight than gold, so ordinary sweetening runs on honey, fruit, and cheaper local substitutes. The far south is cold, and keeps old mountain and island sauna cultures; the most prestigious sauna houses are built of black tide-tree wood — dark, heat-resistant, and near-indestructible — and traditionalists regard the modern Firelit-powered imitations as gauche.

The most popular sport among normal folk is river wrestling, where shirtless men wrestle to see whose shoulders touch the water first - it is viewed as rather barbaric by the upper classes. Rules vary considerably by region, venue, level, and appetite for violence: some circuits play simple first-shoulders-under, while others (the Drenwick circuit among them) run codified hold-and-break systems fought in moving, at-least-knee-deep current, where being taken under for long seconds is part of the show.

Monsters exist — fen creatures, remnant things in old ruins, worse things below. Ordinary people call some of their lingering effects "curses," and the word is fair as folk usage; scholars pointedly do NOT classify these phenomena as resonance, and there is no ninth magic system. They are simply hazards nobody has been funded to explain.

### The Development Grid

For internal consistency the continent is mapped on a four-by-four grid, lettered and numbered from A1 in the northwest to D4 in the southeast. This is a development coordinate system for maintaining geographic consistency, not necessarily a notation printed on in-world maps. Much of the four corner squares is open ocean carrying islands or short coastal projections; the inhabited mass concentrates through the middle columns.

#### A1 — Northwest Ocean
Mostly ocean; only a short, rocky stretch of the continent's northwestern coast enters the square's southeast. Several inhabited fishing islands lie offshore, their people practised at coping with the storm's interference with resonance — the exact nature of that interference is left undefined here. Imperial control is nominal: tribute is light and administration irregular, because the islands offer little economic reason for a firmer grip.

#### B1 — Northern Monsoon Coast
The warm, humid, agriculturally rich northern coast, where warm currents and seasonal monsoons build wide river deltas, treacherous navigation, and extraordinarily productive farmland. River-clan settlements here predate the Empire and were only partly absorbed by it; the Empire admits the region is substantially underregistered. Calivar City sits about forty kilometres inland behind a delta flood buffer — a wealthy, practically semi-independent city that has controlled northern river trade and negotiated with the Empire at arm's length for roughly three centuries, its home-grown grain giving it unusual bargaining power. Its precise constitutional status is undecided. From the northern spits the equatorial storm is at its most visible, a persistent flicker or discoloration above the sea.

#### C1 — Northern Sea and Merovari Coast
Warm still, but more maritime and navigable year-round than B1, as the northern warm current curves northeast and moderates the coast. Merovar is the principal city: a wealthy maritime port whose institutions and outward-looking identity predate the Empire, and whose food supply and sea trade leave it less dependent on the Lume heartland than most great cities. Island chains run north, some of them identifying more with Merovar than with the Empire. Two islands near the centre of the square are the only known sources of sugar cane, and so produce the known world's exceptionally valuable refined sugar.

#### D1 — Northeast Ocean
Mostly ocean, holding only the continent's northeastern peninsula or broken coastal tip. Fishing and trading islands run into the northeastern sea; they are technically Imperial but inconsistently administered, and seasonal storms are common. The commercial routes between Merovar and Stonehaven pass along the square's southern edge, which lends some islands economic weight despite their political marginality.

#### A2 — Western Escarpment and Rain Shadow
The great western spine — roughly 2,000 kilometres long — passes through A2 and on into northern A3. Here it stands as a dramatic uplifted escarpment: moisture falls heavily on its ocean-facing western slopes while its eastern side lies in semi-arid rain shadow, an interior of broken mesas, dry ravines, pale metallic soils, and iron dust that bends light into thread-coloured mirages. Varost is the kingdom of this region — sparsely peopled, proud, suspicious of central authority, and strategically vital because it holds the few viable east–west passes. Redfall stands beside a perennial aquifer spring, the only dependable water for some two hundred kilometres.

#### B2 — Lume Basin Heartland
The agricultural and demographic heartland of the Empire. The Lume rises in the northern Uplands of C3, flows northwest into B2, then bends northeast through the heartland toward Lumina, gathering major tributaries across a broad floodplain. Floods every five to eight years renew the deep loam and also ruin what stands in them; generations of commonborn and fallible engineers have managed the system imperfectly with levees, drainage works, and diversions. Lumina stands in northeastern B2 on its limestone bluff above the Lume. The Brinne joins the Lume southwest of Lumina, and the Ludmereson crater — "the Bowl" — lies at or near that confluence, holding the Junior Academy; the traditional Senior Academy lies farther east, between Lumina and Halcyra. B2 is among the most densely settled and politically weighty parts of the continent.

#### C2 — Cyrmere and the Eastern Basin
The Cyr descends from the northern highlands, runs south, then curves southwest to enter the Cyrmere on its northwestern shore. The Cyrmere is a large freshwater inland sea, about 180 kilometres across at its widest, with island communities, its own regional weather, and a broad commercial ecology. Halcyra stands on its western-to-northwestern shore, beside the Cyr's mouth. The territory around the lake is Cyrren. Eastward the square climbs toward the granite shield. Litorra lies on the Cyrmere's southeastern shore, near where lake shipping transfers to the roads into the shield — a position that suits its role as a practical administrative city famous for courts, clerks, and professional petition-writers.

#### D2 — Northern Eastern Shield
An ancient granite shield: exposed bedrock, coniferous forest, iron and copper, short rivers and steep drops to the coast. Stonehaven stands where one of those rivers reaches a natural harbour — a fortified granite port with a long trade in ships, metal, and weapons, and a standing refusal to name an official civic colour beyond "still grey." Its bond with the Empire is mutually practical: the Empire wants its timber and minerals, Stonehaven wants Imperial markets.

#### A3 — The Arc and Mineral Uplands
The western spine continues into northern A3 and softens from a single escarpment into a broken volcanic and mineral country — the Arc: ridges, mesas, dry ravines, metallic soils, and valleys hazed by iron-dust mirages. Its mercury lake lies in a shallow basin above a cinnabar-rich geothermal system, ringed by metallic fog and a specialized toxic microbiome — an exceptional natural and resonance-geological anomaly rather than an ordinary volcanic lake. Population is thin and gathers in the north; Imperial administration is thinner still, leaving a quiet local autonomy that suits the Reclaimers and unsettles the Registry.

#### B3 — The Fens and Valmere Lowlands
The great southwestern lowland where the game takes place: slow water, reed plains, forests, marshes, canals, seasonal flooding, and several inland waters. The Valmere is the largest regional inland sea and the namesake of the province — far larger than the Thornmere, and lying well east of Calwick. The Thornmere is Calwick and Drenwick's much smaller local sea-and-fen system; locals live on the Thornmere, while the Valmere is "the big sea, way out east" that the canal traffic ultimately serves. Drynport stands in northeastern B3 on the northwestern shore of the Valmere, where major drainage and canal systems meet the lake; fluctuating waters, pinched shorelines, and endless crossings have made it a vertical, bridge-filled, flood-adaptive city, and the major urban centre of the Valmere region. Calwick and Drenwick lie in the sparse marshy southwest of the square, Drenwick immediately east of Calwick; the nearest aetherrail point is two towns further east of Drenwick, and neither town has a station. These fens hold the densest known concentration of pre-Imperial ruins — scholars infer that several important kingdoms stood here before the Century War, but their identities and histories survive only in fragments.

#### C3 — Transitional South and the Uplands
Here the continent begins its turn toward the colder south. The northern Uplands of the square hold the headwaters of the Lume; Teralyn occupies the southeastern Uplands. The Haze Reaches spread across the extreme east of C3 into western D3, and very few people knowingly enter their deepest mists. The Frog Shrine of an unnamed god stands near the border country, roughly fifty kilometres north of the inland end of the Tidewave Chasm; it is visited, though neither its god nor its purpose is understood.

#### D3 — Great East Coast, Tidewave Chasm, and Spindler's Coast
The eastern granite shield runs on south, but the climate cools markedly as the northern current's warmth fades. The Tidewave Chasm is wholly a coastal feature of southwestern D3 — a long, periodically tidal arm of the eastern sea cutting westward into the land without crossing into C3, passable only at two engineered spans; the Haze Reaches wrap its inland approaches and may straddle the C3–D3 line. The Great East Coast is the broad name for this long eastern seaboard, and Spindler's Coast is one named stretch of it, marked by fishing settlements, narrow offshore islands, and reliance on the Silver Vein current. The eastern sea lanes matter commercially — fishing communities, trade waypoints, and culturally distinct islands run offshore — while Imperial administration falls away quickly beyond the granite shield. Merovari and other eastern sailors have attempted long voyages; they found no land within reliable provisioning range, and open-ocean currents and weather make anything more distant prohibitively dangerous. Exploration was attempted, in other words, not merely neglected because the Empire was uninterested.

#### A4 — Southern Outer Ocean
Mostly cold southern ocean, scattered with subpolar islands, seal coasts, and small maritime communities. Because these islands sit in the square's northern portion, they stay somewhat warmer than the land and ice further southeast.

#### B4 — Southern Maritime Belt
A narrow belt of harsh coasts and thin settlement. Farming falls off sharply, maritime subsistence grows more important, and settlement clings to protected bays and river mouths.

#### C4 — Polar Verge and Frostward Reach
The Southern Uplands give way to the Polar Verge across the Palefall Line — an ecological and climatic transition, not a straight political border — that runs across northern C4, roughly along the C3–C4 boundary before bending southeast toward the base of Cape Denial. Three great forts hold the frontier. Fort Orrivar stands in northwestern C4 just south of the Palefall Line, on the principal road down from Teralyn, and serves chiefly as a research and observation station. Fort Drenn stands in south-central C4 around an inland geothermal field whose heat gives dependable liquid water, winter shelter, and a southern supply base. Fort Ariel stands in eastern C4 within the Frostward Reach, near the eastern end of the Palefall Line and west of Cape Denial's root — the most famous and remote of the major Imperial forts: a farming community of some three thousand people living inside a perfectly circular zone of warmth held against the polar cold by ancient resonance no one now understands (see Fort Ariel and the Warm Circle, below).

#### D4 — Cape Denial and the Frozen Southeast Ocean
Mostly frozen southeastern ocean, but Cape Denial reaches into its northern portion — a long eastern promontory south of the Tidewave Chasm. The Silver Vein carries the Great East Coast's last maritime warmth along the cape's northern face before it weakens or turns away; beyond lie ice seas, polar storms, and the barest settlement. The Imperial expedition route reached the south pole around 900 AC, which means the pole is known, not that every eastern and southeastern ice sea has been charted — the immense ocean east and southeast of Cape Denial remains incompletely explored. Because the continent angles southeast, Cape Denial, C4, and northern D4 run much colder than A4 despite sharing the same broad grid row.

### Political and Cartographic Limits

This grid fixes physical geography, not a finished political map. Varost (A2) and Teralyn (C3) may be named among the eight kingdoms; the locations, borders, and identities of the remaining six are deliberately left open. Cyrren is the established region surrounding the Cyrmere. Calivar's practical autonomy is established, but its precise legal status stays undecided. The aetherrail's lines remain concentrated in the richer northeast, as established elsewhere. Do not turn this physical grid into a falsely complete political map.

### Cartographic Guidance

A continent-scale Imperial school map should prioritize coastlines, island chains, major terrain regions, mountain systems, rivers, inland seas, capitals, major cities, the equatorial storm boundary, and the northeastern aetherrail network. Local details — Redfall, the Frog Shrine, individual forts — belong on regional maps or insets. Calwick and Drenwick are too small for ordinary continental prominence, though a Calwick school map may reasonably add a local "you are here" marker.

### Fort Ariel and the Warm Circle

Fort Ariel lies deep within the Frostward Reach — the Southern Frost Reach — hundreds of kilometres from any naturally habitable land, and by rights should not exist at all. It was raised shortly before the Century War as **Fort Arrhall**, the last permanent staging post of an old kingdom's southern expedition — built on a rare shelf of stable bedrock with access to meltwater, and serving as supply depot, refuge, observation station, and assertion of territorial possession over the Reach. **Arrhall** led the expedition that gave the fort its name, though the surviving records do not agree on his ultimate fate. The expedition never reached the south pole, yet the fort was not abandoned: rather than surrender the claim, successive governments kept supplying it, and temporary postings hardened into a permanent community of officials, labourers, and families. By the time Ariel was born its ambitions had dwindled to an expensive, largely purposeless institutional inheritance — but it was home.

Home, above all, to a child. A Prismborn girl named **Ariel** was born and raised at the fort, and by every account she simply loved the place — her cold, isolated town — and, in the way of children, she wanted fresh strawberries.

What Ariel did in answer is the most studied and least understood act of resonance on record. She performed some working that laid a perfectly circular zone of warmth over the fort, and it has never stopped. More than eleven centuries later her magic is still actively holding back the polar cold — not geothermal heat, not machinery anyone tends, not a spent charge fading, but an ongoing act that has outlived its maker by a thousand years. Modern scholars cannot say which resonance she used, what she actually created, how it sustains itself, or why it has never measurably weakened. The knowledge needed to understand it, let alone reproduce it, is simply lost.

The habitable zone is a disc of radius exactly six kilometres. The inner four-kilometre radius is prime agricultural land, warm enough to grow almost any temperate crop — strawberries among them. From four to six kilometres the climate cools rapidly but still carries cereals: wheat, rye, barley, oats. Farms follow the thermal bands rather than any surveyor's line, so their outer fields have visibly curved edges, and at six kilometres the warmth ends sharply — a step across that line and nearly lifeless polar frost runs on for hundreds of kilometres. Roughly three to three and a half thousand people live inside the circle. Their farms largely feed them, with land to spare for pasture, woodland, water, wildlife, and fallow.

Ariel's warmth controls temperature but does nothing to the polar sky. The fort still keeps the latitude's light: only three or four months of strong farming light, then eight or nine months of twilight, true polar night, and slowly returning grey. Summer is joyful and frantic — planting, the strawberry harvest, festivals, and the drying, milling, pickling, and storing that must carry the town through the long warm dark.

Several fungi evolved nowhere but inside the circle, and are the town's second harvest, conjuring no energy from nothing but consuming and recycling the enormous mass of roots, straw, manure, and dead vegetation that summer leaves behind. Warmdark caps are cultivated for food, giving protein, B vitamins, and minerals. Ariel's cups, dark red and unusually rich in fermentable carbohydrates, make the fort's distinctive tart, earthy mushroom wine. Frostlace grows in the outer ring's cereal stubble, dried or ground to enrich winter meals. The fungi fruit hardest in autumn and spring, and those dim shoulder seasons are also the hunting seasons — hares, small deer, waterfowl, burrowing animals, and the large insects endemic to the circle. Autumn hunting lays in food against the darkness; spring hunting guards the new plantings. The year turns in four movements: brilliant summer farming and preservation; falling-light harvest, fungi, and the hunt; the long warm darkness sustained on stored food and cultivated mushrooms; and the returning-light blooms, hunts, repairs, and preparation for planting.

The people of Fort Ariel know perfectly well that their whole world rests on a magic no one alive understands, and it does not haunt them. The warmth has held through the whole of local memory, and they trust it as anyone trusts the seasons to turn. Nothing survives to say how Ariel did it — only the circle itself, still warm, still exactly six kilometres across. She must simply have loved her little town very much.

## Civic Culture: The Petition of Mera Dren

Mera Dren was a young woman from Kaith's Reach, a remote settlement in Deltratha. When an Imperial road project threatened to block the village's traditional route to its water source, Mera invoked Civil Statute 97-B, Subpart C — the Water Access Clause. With help from neighbours and a local clerk, she submitted measurements, witness marks and a plainly argued petition to the proper regional office. An engineer confirmed the problem, a supervisor authorized the correction, and six weeks later the road was moved thirty paces west. The petition genuinely worked.

Within a generation, the Empire transformed the case into one of its favourite civic lessons. Schoolchildren study simplified versions of Mera's petition, civil-service examinations quote it, and pamphlets present her as patient, respectful, precise and unfailingly loyal. Expressions such as "Be a Mera" and "by the clause" praise citizens who pursue grievances through proper procedure. Official illustrations invariably show her seated at a modest table with statute and pen, never confronting officials or expressing anger.

The official account minimizes that Imperial planners caused the problem, that most villagers lacked Mera's literacy and assistance, and that many equally deserving petitions disappear into administrative silence. It also removes the clerk, neighbours and engineer whose cooperation made the correction possible. Mera remained proud of what she accomplished and believed others should learn to use the law, but grew uncomfortable with being treated as proof that the Empire always listens. She is later reported to have said: "I proved that it listened once."

The story's power lies in its truth. The Empire uses Mera to imply that institutional failures result from citizens not following the correct procedure — but the lesson can still help another neglected community save a well, field or footpath. Its propaganda succeeds partly because it offers genuinely useful knowledge.

## Tone

The Empire is neither good nor bad — genuinely beneficial in many ways, genuinely aging and corrupt in others. No chosen ones. No ancient evil. The world in 1072 AC is five steps from the precipice, not one. The primary everyday concerns are population growth, education, healthcare, political representation — normal problems of a civilization doing well enough to have arrived at the next tier of difficulties. The trains are still marvel for those who get to ride them. The people drink mushroom wine on dayoff. Ten million people living their lives.

## The Game

Browser-based old school JRPG. Player character is a junior IJC Investigator posted to Calwick, a wetlands town of ~800 on the margin of the Thornmere, a cool, small, shallow sea-and-fen system southwest of the capitals' region. (The province is named for the Valmere, the far larger inland sea well to the east — see The World; the Valmere is not local to Calwick.) Calwick's economy: reed harvesting, fishing, agricultural drainage management, ruins documentation when structures emerge from the mud in dry years. Second town: Drenwick, population ~2000, larger and more impersonal. A canal town that will be severely economically impacted if the drought goes on for too long.

As the game starts, the water situation has three distinct layers: the North Basin reservoir system has been quietly declining for years (old waterlines stranded well above the water, mooring posts on dry ground); the current dry spell has lasted three rainless months; and only now has the local office officially classified it as a drought. The cause is unknown. Nobody's dying, but people are starting to get nervous.

## The Calwick Office

The IJC district office at Calwick is small: a supervisor, a rareborn registration officer who doubles as his deputy, and a handful of records and payments clerks. Their histories explain their voices. (The dates use the AC calendar; the current year is approximately 1072 AC. The player-character investigator is referred to here as "the investigator.")

### Esla Jonbur — Rareborn Registration Officer

Born approximately 1048 AC into a conservative outlands family; her green hair marked her Bloommarked at birth. Her mother registered her young and signed the Alecton prep-school forms before Esla was two — a quiet pride the two of them never spoke plainly about. Her father never accepted what she was. She boarded much of her early childhood at Alecton, but visits home stayed dangerous, and at about ten her father attempted to drown her. Her mother responded by surrendering her into emergency Imperial conservatorship rather than let her return home; Esla spent the following two years in Halcyra as a "child of the continent" before entering the Academy at twelve. She graduated at eighteen and asked for "somewhere quiet." The placement office sent her to Calwick.

Six years on she is the station's second-highest officer — young for the post, but the Academy and Registry deliberately accelerate capable rareborn into specialized work; she was offered a transfer to a larger Registry office at the end of her first year and declined it. Her responsibilities are rareborn registration, three-year reviews, census classifications and correspondence with the central Registry, and she stands in as the supervisor's deputy when needed. Living with Tomas has connected her to Calwick in a way no placement order could — he does not fully understand the Academy, but he understands her habits, her silences, and her preferred soup. She is observant because noticing danger once mattered enormously; she trusts Imperial procedure because procedure saved her life and distrusts it because the same system classified and controlled her; her dry humour lets her acknowledge absurdity without becoming exposed; and she finds the fen's plants, fungi and insects more legible than people.

### The Supervisor — District Investigations

Unnamed for now; forty-eight. He grew up in a respectable but unimportant civic household in a provincial district seat — his father inspected roads and culverts, his mother taught in an Imperial school. Neither possessed influence, but both understood that examinations and dependable service could give their children stable lives. He entered Imperial service at twenty, writing incident summaries for a regional works office, and transferred into field investigation after coordinating the evacuation of several settlements during a serious flood — not heroic in the conventional sense, but able to read contradictory reports, identify which embankment would fail first, and send people the correct way. That was exactly the competence the investigative service wanted. He arrived in Calwick in 1058 AC as an experienced field officer and inherited the supervisor's desk four years later; his "fourteen years" is that tenure. The years have made him economical with both words and alarm — he has seen apparent emergencies prove clerical and "routine inspections" produce bodies. He sends the investigator into danger because it is genuinely her job, but he never pretends that signing the resulting file absolves him. His formality is partly discipline: if he let every case become personal, he could not continue assigning them.

### Aldric — Requisitions & Records

Fifty-six. He came to Calwick twenty-four years ago and has worked in its Imperial office for twenty-two, so he knows the station better than the supervisor while remaining well below him in rank — a distinction Aldric considers entirely normal. He was raised near a fortified road post, where his father maintained the equipment stores and his mother repaired uniforms and canvas goods; he learned early that missing tools, inaccurate inventories and badly maintained gear could kill people as effectively as monsters. He began as a depot tally clerk, passed the civic examinations, and moved into requisitions and records — Calwick needed one person for both its small equipment issue and its correspondence archive, and the posting he expected to last perhaps five years did not. He never pursued management: he likes problems that can be closed properly and dislikes meetings about whether they should be closed. His stubbornness explains the three unanswered reports about monsters carrying sorted, minted gold — being ignored does not make the observation incorrect — and his pleasure when a two-month discrepancy receives a "clean finish" is completely sincere.

### Petra — Cashier & Expenditure Clerk

Thirty-five, born in Calwick; entered the Imperial office twelve years ago. Her parents operated a modest market stall dealing in preserved fish, grain and household supplies, and she grew up counting coins, checking weights, and watching how a single missing payment could affect a family's week. She performed exceptionally well in arithmetic at the Imperial school and later passed the civic accounts examination; the Empire offered something the stall could not — a predictable salary and work that did not depend directly on harvests, catches or floods. She began as a temporary payments assistant and gradually became the station's effective cashier and expenditure clerk, processing pay tickets, reimbursements and local disbursements and reconciling the physical gold against her register. She expresses moral discomfort through accounting language: when a dangerous assignment produces a two-hundred-gold payment, she does not ask what happened, she wonders which column such a payment belongs in. Numbers are how she approaches experiences that otherwise feel too large.

### Corvin — Reconciliation Clerk

Forty. He grew up in a working canal family near Drenwick and moved to Calwick nine years ago, roughly fifteen years into his Imperial employment. His father tallied barge loads while his mother took seasonal work supplying and laundering for canal crews; Corvin worked the docks first, loading cargo and recording weights, with little affection for formal schooling but an excellent instinct for when two numbers ought to match. After he identified a persistent discrepancy caused by a damaged district scale, a canal officer encouraged him to sit the internal service examination; he passed narrowly, became a reconciliation clerk, and was eventually recruited to Calwick because its water, trade and works records routinely arrive from several offices in incompatible formats. He retains the clothing, posture and language of someone who came into administration from manual work rather than through a prestigious school. He finds bureaucracy ridiculous but understands that an accurate record may be the only evidence a worker has that something happened; numbers comfort him because they can be corrected, while deaths, frightened families and morally ambiguous reports cannot.
